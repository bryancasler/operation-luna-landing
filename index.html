Good to know

How to read the numbers

Every building shows what a month really costs in year one, under each situation that applies: with the free months the listing advertises, and with no deal at all. Here's my math: twelve months of rent, minus any free months. Then I add pet rent (capped by DC law at 1 percent of rent per dog), any required building fees, and the utilities you'd pay yourself (about $120 a month for a studio when nothing is included). One-time fees get spread across the year, non-refundable pet fees are left out because DC law bans them, and I leave refundable deposits out since you get them back. Under each building, the deal line says whether that special was on the listing this week, was advertised earlier this year, or shows up only on third-party sites. Where I had to guess, a short note says so.

For the income-restricted buildings, the first line is a studio at the lowest income tier, where rent is capped near $1,240 with utilities. That only happens if you win the lottery.

The review score blends every site that publishes both a star rating and a review count for the building, weighted by how many reviews each has. I pulled Google fresh on September 15, 2026 for every building, and folded in ApartmentRatings, Yelp, and Apartments.com where they publish a score. ApartmentRatings blocks automated reading, so its numbers come from what its listing pages show in search results; a few of its scores are survey-based rather than written reviews, and the card says so where that matters.

Small samples get less credit. I pull each raw rating toward the list average (3.9 stars) as if 30 average reviews were added to it. A 5.0 on 13 reviews lands at 4.2; a 4.7 on 76 reviews holds at 4.5. Past a few dozen reviews the pull barely matters. It's the same idea IMDb uses for its Top 250: reviews times raw rating, plus 30 times the list average, divided by reviews plus 30.

I set the filters to start at $2,000 or less with no discounts, a score of 4.0 or better, and at least 50 reviews. Loosen them to see the rest. The score-against-cost chart, behind the toggle above the map, plots every priced building no matter the filters, except ones you've hidden. Hiding is for buildings you've toured and ruled out. They stay hidden until you tick "Show the buildings you've hidden" or unhide them when you reset.

All review figures are all-time. Reviews from before 2023 say little about how a building runs today, and Google only shows review dates inside its own app, so my advice is to open the Maps link, sort by newest, and read back to September 2023.

Winter is when to sign

January is usually the cheapest month to sign a lease in DC; August and September are the most expensive. Your window runs to mid-January, so use it: tour in November, sign for a December or January move-in, and ask every building what winter deal is running and how it's applied. The one exception: if a unit you like is the last of its type in the building, take it.

Before you apply, ask every building
  1. Do you take dogs at all? Get it in writing. For a lease starting after October 1, 2026, DC Law 25-308 means no weight cap, breed list, or size rule can apply, so don't let the conversation start from Luna's weight.
  2. Pet deposit (must be refundable and under 15 percent of a month's rent), no non-refundable pet fee, and pet rent at no more than 1 percent of the first month's rent per dog. Ask for the pet addendum to say so.
  3. Every fee: application, admin, move-in, amenity, security deposit. DC caps the security deposit at one month's rent, and it has to come back within 45 days of moving out, with interest.
  4. Which utilities are included. If electric isn't, what did the last tenant pay on average?
  5. How the free months work: spread across the lease or taken up front? Minimum lease length? Move-in-by date?
  6. Is renter's insurance required before key pickup?
  7. Any pest treatments in the last year, and how packages are handled.
  8. Which studios are expected to open for a December or January move-in, at what rent, and what winter deal will be running by then?

Each building in the list also has one to three questions I'd ask only there.

A new DC law helps Luna

DC Law 25-308, the Pets in Housing Amendment Act, changes the dog math for any lease you sign. Once it's in force, a building that allows pets can't charge a non-refundable pet fee, has to keep any pet deposit refundable and under 15 percent of a month's rent, and can't charge more than 1 percent of the first month's rent per dog in pet rent. For leases starting after October 1, 2026, which covers a December or January move-in, it also can't restrict tenancy or charge more based on a pet's breed, size, or weight.

Two limits on that. A building can still refuse pets entirely, and it can still cap the number of pets. So the only dog question that matters now is whether a building takes dogs at all. Every weight cap on this page is void for a lease starting after October 1, 2026, the costs on each card already use the legal caps, and the reopened buildings (The Woodner, Oaklawn, Gelmarc Towers, Louis at 14th) are flagged. Get the compliant terms written into the lease or pet addendum, and if a leasing office pushes back, point them to the law by number.

If a leasing office says the law isn't in effect, here's the history. The act became DC Law 25-308 on March 21, 2025, but the whole thing carried a "subject to appropriations" clause and wasn't funded in the FY2026 budget, so through mid-2026 none of it was enforceable and the code sections were marked "Not Funded." That's what property managers were telling people, and they were right at the time. It changed over the summer. The Fiscal Year 2027 Budget Support Act of 2026 clarified that the funding condition applies only to the pet-friendly homeless shelter the law also requires, not to the rules for landlords, so the landlord rules take effect October 1, 2026. The Council passed it 13 to 0 on July 7, 2026, the Mayor let it become law without her signature, it was enacted as Act A26-0418 on August 14 and published in the DC Register on August 21, and it's finishing the routine congressional review period. Its emergency twin, the Fiscal Year 2027 Budget Support Emergency Act (Act A26-0416), was enacted August 13 and runs through November 11, 2026, which is how DC bridges the gap while the permanent act clears review.

What to say on the phone: "I know the 2024 pet law was unfunded, but the FY2027 Budget Support Act fixed that, and the landlord provisions apply to leases starting after October 1, 2026." Then ask for the pet addendum to reflect it. The law is enforced through the Rent Administrator and DC Superior Court, so a lease term that breaks it is unenforceable against you, but it's far easier to get it right on paper up front than to argue later. Sources: the DC Law Library text of Law 25-308, the DC Council's legislative record for Bills 26-661 and 26-724, and a July 22, 2026 analysis by the DC landlord-side firm Greenstein DeLorme & Luchs.

Income-restricted studios

Income-restricted units are apartments in newer buildings set aside for people under certain income levels, mostly through DC's Inclusionary Zoning program (IZ for short). They're the one way to get a studio well under $1,500 in this part of town. You can find them online, but the process runs on its own clock, so I'd treat this as a side track, not the plan.

  1. Search DC Housing Search. dchousingsearch.org is the District's free listing site, and every affordable unit is required to be posted there. Filter for studios in 20009 and 20010 and look for the red "R" (income restricted) and the blue and white "IZ" icon. Set up the email alerts. Anything marked "Non-lottery IZ/ADU" takes applications directly, with no lottery.
  2. Register for the IZ lottery. Take the free two-hour orientation (virtual sessions on the DHCD schedule), then register at izhousingdc.org. After that, the housing department (DHCD) emails you when a unit matching your income and household size opens up, and you enter that unit's lottery. One-person households qualify for studios and one-bedrooms. Program details are on the DHCD IZ page; questions go to iz.adu@dc.gov or 202-442-7221.
  3. Ask the buildings directly. Buildings with IZ units say so on their own sites, usually with the line "income restricted units are available at this development." Five near the park are in the list, each with the gold "Affordable only through the income-restricted lottery" pill: View 14, Capitol View on 14th, Collection 14, Highland Park, and Louis at 14th. Each card says where it stands on dogs and what to ask. The Harper on 14th also has IZ units but is pet-free. Shaw has the densest cluster: The Wren has 132 units at the 30 and 50 percent tiers, The Shay and Trellis House have some, and 901W and Atlantic Plumbing have theirs only at the 80 percent tier, where the rent cap is about $2,010, so they don't help with this budget.
  4. Two more places to look. Apartments.com keeps an income-restricted studios filter for DC, and the District publishes a map of every subsidized and IZ building, useful for spotting older affordable buildings that never show up on the big listing sites.

The 2026 limits for a one-person household are higher than most people expect:

The catch is timing. Lotteries happen only when a unit opens, there's no schedule, and DC's auditor found the program taking more than 13 months on average to fill a unit, against a 102-day target. I'd register anyway. It costs nothing, the certificate lasts two years, and if a studio at the 50 or 60 percent tier comes up the rent would be about $1,080 to $1,360 plus utilities. The DC Housing Authority voucher waitlist has been closed since 2013, so skip that one.

Walk away if
  • The rent is far below market and the landlord can't show you the unit.
  • Anyone asks you to wire money or pay by Zelle or gift card before you've toured and signed.
  • There's no street address, or the photos don't match the address.
  • You're pressured to pay a holding fee on the spot.

My rule: stick to buildings you can find on the management company's own site.
